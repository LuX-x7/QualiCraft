Interviewer: Thanks for making the time. I would like to hear about how your studying changed once the course moved online. Start wherever you like.

Participant: Before the move I had a routine I never had to think about. I walked to the library at nine, sat with the same three people, and left at six. When the course went online that structure disappeared overnight and I did not replace it for weeks. The first few weeks were loud and empty at the same time. Loud, because everything was suddenly happening in the same room, and empty, because I was not speaking to anyone about the material.

Interviewer: Was the isolation the first thing you noticed?

Participant: It was the last thing I noticed, which is odd to say now. At first I thought I was just being lazy. The work suffered before the feeling did. I would read something and have no way of knowing whether I had understood it. In a seminar you find that out in about four minutes. Online, I could go a whole week believing I understood something I had got wrong, and nothing in the week would contradict me.

Interviewer: How did you handle the schedule once it was entirely yours to set?

Participant: Badly for a while. I treated every hour as available, which meant no hour was. Then I started blocking the day the way a timetable would, with the same subject at the same time, and that helped more than anything else I tried. The course gave deadlines and nothing else. The schedule was mine to invent, and inventing it took energy I would rather have spent on the reading. You are not just doing the degree, you are also running the small business of doing the degree.

Interviewer: Did the schedule ever break down?

Participant: Every few weeks. Something would slip and two days would go nowhere, and then I would spend a day rebuilding the plan instead of doing the work. What helped was deciding in advance that this would happen, so it stopped feeling like a failure. Mornings were for reading, afternoons for writing, and the evening for whatever I had avoided. I kept the evenings deliberately vague, because a schedule with no slack collapses the first time something goes wrong.

Interviewer: Tell me about the feedback, because that came up earlier.

Participant: The feedback was the thing I missed most. When you write an essay and get it back a month later with two lines at the bottom, the timing makes it nearly useless. By then you have moved on to the next thing. When the course was online the turnaround got worse, not better. I asked for more feedback twice and got a polite reply both times, but asking feels like a complaint when everyone is busy, so I stopped. That is the isolation again, in a different form.

Interviewer: How did you know whether you were doing well?

Participant: I did not, mostly. That is the honest answer. I could tell whether I had submitted things, and I could tell roughly what marks I had, but a mark is not the same as knowing whether you are getting better at the subject.

Interviewer: Did you use the forums, or any group chat?

Participant: I used one group chat with four people from my first year, and that kept me going. What it did not do was help with the actual work, because we were all stuck at the same points and none of us had the answer. So the schedule held, but the feedback did not. The schedule held because I forced it to. The feedback never arrived in a form I could use, so I built my own out of comparing my essays with each other. That is a poor substitute and I knew it at the time.

Interviewer: What did you do when a topic would not go in?

Participant: I went back to the recordings. Having the lecture available a second time was the one clear advantage. I could stop it, write down what confused me, and rewind. I could not do that in a lecture theatre, so in that one respect the change helped. But a recording answers the question you already know how to ask. It cannot tell you that your question is the wrong one, and that is where the isolation does its damage.

Interviewer: Was there a point where you thought about stopping?

Participant: Around the middle of the second term, yes. Not because the work was too hard, but because I could not see the shape of it. Everything was one long week after another with no edges. The schedule gave me edges back.

Interviewer: Did anything get easier over the year?

Participant: Yes, but slowly, and not for the reason I expected. I thought I would get better at studying online. What actually happened is that I got better at tolerating the uncertainty. The feedback still did not arrive. I stopped waiting for it, and that was a kind of progress, though not the kind anyone would put in a prospectus.

Interviewer: Tell me about a week that went well.

Participant: There was one in the third term. I had a schedule I trusted, I had a seminar group of five that met on Thursdays, and I got an essay back in nine days. It was the same work as before. It felt completely different. The feedback came early enough to matter, and there were four other people who had read the same thing. That combination is the whole answer, I think. The schedule alone is not enough and the feedback alone is not enough.

Interviewer: If you were designing the course, what would you change first?

Participant: Fixed times for discussion, even short ones. Half an hour a week where you know four other people are in a room with you, online. And feedback that comes back inside two weeks, even if it is shorter. Timeliness matters more than length.

Interviewer: You have mentioned the schedule several times. Is that the thing you would protect?

Participant: Yes. If I had to give one piece of advice it would be that. Build the schedule before you need it. The isolation is easier to survive when you know what you are doing at eleven o'clock.

Interviewer: If the course moved back to in-person tomorrow, what would you keep?

Participant: The recordings, and the habit of writing down what confused me before asking anyone. Those two things made me better at the subject rather than just better at coping with it.

Interviewer: Anything I should have asked and did not?

Participant: I would ask about the people who did not have a group chat. I had four people and I got through. I do not know what I would have done with none, and I think that is the difference that decides whether someone finishes.
